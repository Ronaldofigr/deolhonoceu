According to new research from the University of Portsmouth, the building blocks of rocky planets may have begun to form just 100 million years after the Big Bang — long before the first galaxies even existed.

Scientists had previously assumed that planet formation was a slow process that only truly got going several billion years after the start of the Universe’s history, but a new study has found that it happened much earlier than thought.

Some of the first stars in the Universe were enormous and ended their lives in powerful explosions. These explosions scattered huge amounts of the chemical elements needed for planet formation — such as carbon, oxygen and iron — into the surrounding gas.

These explosions, known as Population III supernovae, were the Universe’s first factories for the elements needed to form planets and, ultimately, life.

Dr Daniel Whalen of the Institute of Cosmology and Gravitation at the University of Portsmouth said: “Our new scientific paper shows that the precursors of terrestrial planets can form around long-lived, low-mass stars in the debris of the first cosmic explosions, 100 million years after the Big Bang. To put that in context, the Universe is about 13.8 billion years old, so this corresponds to an extremely early phase of cosmic history.”

A specific type of Population III supernova — called a pair-instability supernova — is so powerful that it can eject into space, in a single explosion, more than 100 times the mass of the Sun in heavy elements. This material can enrich nearby gas clouds, leading to surprisingly high concentrations of heavy elements. Those enriched clouds can then collapse under gravity and form a rotating disk of material around a young star.

In the simulations the researchers found one such disk around a young star with about 70 percent of the Sun’s mass. Inside that disk, enough solid material accumulated to create planetary building blocks equivalent to several Earth masses, at roughly the same distance from the star as the Earth is from the Sun.

Most surprisingly, the disk also contained substantial amounts of water, only a few times lower than what was available when our own Solar System formed. This means that any planets that formed there could potentially have received water in a similar way to Earth.

“Our findings suggest that the conditions for planet formation may have existed much earlier than previously thought. If that is the case, it raises an intriguing question: could potentially habitable worlds also have arisen much earlier in the history of the Universe?”

The scientific paper was published in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*.
