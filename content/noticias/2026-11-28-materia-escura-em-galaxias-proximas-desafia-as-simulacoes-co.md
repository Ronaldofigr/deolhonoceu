The inner regions of nearby galaxies contain less dark matter than some of the most advanced cosmological simulations predict. A study of 136 galaxies, involving the Instituto de Astrofísica de Canarias (IAC), reveals a discrepancy that becomes more extensive in more massive systems and offers fresh clues about how galaxies form and evolve.

Led by Yu Lei of the Shanghai Astronomical Observatory, the team combined measurements of stellar motions from the MaNGA and CALIFA surveys with radio observations of neutral hydrogen, including data from the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical radio Telescope (FAST). Together these tracers map the gravitational field from galaxy centres to their distant outskirts. The work is published in the journal Nature Astronomy.

The sample includes galaxies of different shapes and sizes, with stellar masses ranging from about 1 billion to 300 billion solar masses. Modelling the motions of stars and gas together allowed the researchers to disentangle the contributions of visible matter and dark matter out to distances of around 20–50 kiloparsecs.

The comparison revealed a persistent mismatch. In galaxies with stellar masses above about 10 billion solar masses, the inner dark matter densities are systematically lower than predicted by several widely used simulations, including IllustrisTNG, EAGLE and SIMBA. The region over which the discrepancy appears grows with galaxy mass: from roughly 10 kiloparsecs to around 50 kiloparsecs in the most massive systems, equivalent to about 160,000 light-years.

Many simulations predict that, as stars and gas accumulate toward a galaxy’s centre, their gravity draws dark matter inward, increasing its density. These observations suggest that the balance between processes that concentrate dark matter and those that redistribute it is still not fully captured by the models. The study establishes a demanding observational benchmark for the next generation of simulations.
