An international team of astronomers has identified the faintest supernova ever observed. SN 2024vjm, detected by the BlackGEM array in Chile in the spiral galaxy NGC 6744 about 30 million light-years away, produced less than 1% of the light of a typical supernova and yet remained visible for nearly a year.

Its peak absolute brightness reached about −12.7 magnitudes — hundreds of times fainter than a classical Type Ia supernova. The spectrum revealed a gas cloud expanding at only around 3,000 km/s, far slower than the more than 10,000 km/s usual for supernovae. The composition of the material is identical to that of Type Ia supernovae, which are associated with the destruction of a white dwarf.

Images from the Euclid space telescope, taken 11 months before the explosion, show no bright star at the location. Calculations indicate that the progenitor could not have been a luminous star. The explosion most likely originated in a binary system of faint stars — a white dwarf and a dim companion, or possibly a pair of white dwarfs.

The weakness of the explosion kept the gas cloud compact for more than 70 days, trapping the high-energy radiation from radioactive nickel. Only afterwards did the cloud become diffuse enough for the radiation to escape, explaining the long-lasting glow. The discovery suggests that white dwarfs much less massive than the Chandrasekhar limit can also explode and that part of this population has been systematically missed in previous surveys.

The result has implications for the evolution of binary systems and for the use of Type Ia supernovae as “standard candles” in measuring the expansion of the Universe. The team continues to search for similar events with BlackGEM. The study was published in the journal Nature Astronomy.
