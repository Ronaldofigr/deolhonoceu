Em 2015 e 2016, imagens New Horizons das porções mais a norte desta região revelaram células de convecção geológica do tamanho de uma cidade em Sputnik Planitia, separadas por características finas, lineares e escuras, e outras mais difusas e escuras. Novas análises indicam agora que estas características escuras, lineares e difusas parecem ser ocasionalmente e temporariamente molhadas, talvez de vez em quando, por um líquido — muito provavelmente azoto líquido. Este resultado foi publicado na revista científica *The Planetary Science Journal*.

As condições atmosféricas e térmicas de Plutão tornam a chuva de azoto líquido fisicamente impossível. No entanto, os padrões da superfície na parte norte de Sputnik Planitia apresentam zonas escurecidas que se assemelham a características glaciais na Terra que foram molhadas por chuva ou pela ascensão de líquidos do subsolo para a superfície.

As imagens de Sputnik Planitia captadas pela New Horizons são muito semelhantes, sugerindo que líquidos subterrâneos, especificamente azoto, estão a subir e a molhar o gelo de azoto de Plutão.

A new study led by the Southwest Research Institute (SwRI) presents evidence that liquid nitrogen is rising to Pluto's surface through cracks at the northern edge of Sputnik Planitia, part of the enormous heart-shaped glacier on the dwarf planet's surface. This is the first evidence that liquid has recently flowed on Pluto. The study is based on data from NASA's New Horizons probe and is led by SwRI Associate Vice President Dr. Alan Stern, principal investigator of the New Horizons mission.

"Pluto never ceases to surprise us," said lead author Stern, "and this new result is certainly a surprise. In addition to suggesting that there has recently been liquid on Pluto's surface, it also points to a new type of time-variable feature on Pluto."

Sputnik Planitia is a vast glacier of frozen nitrogen on Pluto, larger than Spain and Germany combined. In 2015 and 2016, New Horizons images of the northernmost portions of this region revealed city-sized geological convection cells in Sputnik Planitia, separated by thin, linear, dark features and other more diffuse dark features. New analyses now indicate that these dark, linear and diffuse features appear to be occasionally and temporarily wetted, perhaps from time to time, by a liquid — most likely liquid nitrogen. The result was published in *The Planetary Science Journal*.

Pluto's atmospheric and thermal conditions make liquid nitrogen rain physically impossible. However, the surface patterns in the northern part of Sputnik Planitia show darkened zones that resemble glacial features on Earth that have been wetted by rain or by the rise of liquids from the subsurface to the surface.

The Pluto research team led by SwRI compared New Horizons images with NASA Landsat 9 images of locations on Earth, including the Greenland ice sheet. There, dark, narrow surface features were identified in areas where liquid water occurs in ice and snow. The New Horizons images of Sputnik Planitia are very similar, suggesting that subsurface liquids, specifically nitrogen, are rising and wetting Pluto's nitrogen ice.

"The surface of Sputnik Planitia is quite young, probably less than a million years old based on surface renewal modeling, and so these features we are observing must have formed since then," said Dr. Kelsi Singer, SwRI principal scientist and co-author of the study.

Computer models led by Dr. Orkan Umurhan, senior researcher at the SETI Institute, show that nitrogen ice at the base of Pluto's Sputnik glacier, kilometers deep, can melt, forming liquid nitrogen. Furthermore, these models revealed that this liquid can be transported upward to the surface through small conduits, similar to lava tubes or geysers, driven by buoyancy or pressure from below. Once it reaches the surface, the melted nitrogen can remain liquid long enough to flow down the slopes of the Sputnik glacier, wetting the icy surface and producing the observed dark features.

"I think the great significance of these findings constitutes a strong motivation to continue investigating the physics of solid nitrogen at very low temperatures," said Umurhan. "These processes have never been studied in detail in the laboratory."

Although no other region of Pluto shows evidence of basal flow, more than half of the planet remains unmapped at high resolution. The melting process and upward movement of liquid on Pluto could also explain other phenomena observed in the Solar System, such as the geysers observed by NASA's Voyager 2 probe on Neptune's moon Triton.
