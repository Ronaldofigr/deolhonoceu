Astrónomos do International Space Science Institute anunciaram a 13 de outubro de 2026 que os sistemas planetários parecem dividir-se em uma de duas famílias, consoante a estrela hospedeira. Após estudar 300 dos sistemas multiplaneta melhor caracterizados da Via Láctea, concluíram que estrelas menos massivas tendem a formar sistemas planetários menores em massa e largura, mas com mais planetas. Por outro lado, estrelas mais massivas tendem a formar sistemas mais caóticos e largos, com planetas mais massivos, embora em menor número.

Os investigadores publicaram o estudo na revista *Nature Astronomy* a 13 de outubro de 2026.

A estrela hospedeira de um sistema planetário parece determinar como os seus planetas se formam e evoluem. A quantidade de material na nuvem de gás e poeira que forma a estrela influencia o tipo de família planetária resultante.

Os sistemas planetários compactos e de menor massa tendem a orbitar estrelas tão massivas ou menos massivas do que o Sol. Estas estrelas também tendem a ter quantidades semelhantes ou inferiores de elementos pesados. A largura do sistema é menor e tem planetas menos massivos, mas mais densamente empacotados. Em compensação, tende a ter mais planetas do que o sistema mais massivo.

Os cientistas afirmam que os sistemas de menor massa têm planetas vizinhos semelhantes entre si — o que chamam de configuração “ervilhas numa vagem” (peas in a pod). Estes sistemas têm menos agitação gravitacional e são mais ordenados. Em resumo: estrelas hospedeiras tão ou menos massivas que o Sol, com metalicidade semelhante ou inferior; planetas menos massivos e mais semelhantes entre si; órbitas compactas, próximas da estrela; história dinâmica menos violenta e mais próxima da do Sistema Solar.

Os sistemas planetários mais largos têm estrelas mais massivas do que o Sol, com maior proporção de elementos pesados. Isso resulta em planetas mais massivos, embora em menor número. Estes mundos maiores podem interagir gravitacionalmente, causando instabilidades. Os planetas que sobrevivem a essas interações tendem a ter órbitas mais variadas. Em resumo: estrelas hospedeiras mais massivas que o Sol, com mais elementos pesados; planetas em menor número mas mais massivos; órbitas mais largas e variadas; história dinâmica violenta, com possíveis colisões e ejeções.

Os sistemas mais largos são mais violentos e caóticos, com colisões e ejeções planetárias. Danae Polychroni, do Instituto Nacional de Astrofísica italiano, explicou: “Os cenários que melhor reproduzem as propriedades dinâmicas da família de sistemas largos e massivos são aqueles em que os sistemas são instáveis desde o nascimento. A maior abundância de material a partir do qual os planetas se formam em torno das suas estrelas faz com que nasçam demasiado grandes, impedindo que o sistema permaneça estável.”

Astronomers from the International Space Science Institute said on October 13, 2026, that planetary systems appear to fall into one of two families depending on their home star. After studying 300 of the best-characterized planetary systems in the Milky Way galaxy, they found that less massive stars tend to form planetary systems that are smaller in mass and width but with more planets. On the other hand, more massive stars tend to form planetary systems that are more chaotic and wider with more massive, though fewer, planets.

The researchers published their study in the peer-reviewed journal *Nature Astronomy* on October 13, 2026.

The researchers said that a planetary system’s host star appears to determine how its planets form and evolve. The amount of material in the cloud of gas and dust that forms the star influences what type of planetary family results.

The compact, lower-mass planetary systems tend to orbit stars that are as massive as or less massive than our sun. These stars also tend to have similar or lower amounts of heavy elements. The width of the planetary system is smaller and it has less massive planets, but they’re packed more tightly. But not everything about this family type is diminutive. It tends to have more planets than the more massive planetary system.

Scientists said the lower-mass systems have neighboring planets that are similar to each other, or what they call “peas in a pod.” These systems have less gravitational upheaval and more orderly systems. To sum up: host stars as massive as or less massive than our sun, with similar or lower amounts of heavy elements; planets less massive and more similar to each other; orbits compact, close to the host star; dynamic history less violent and closer to our solar system.

The wider planetary systems have stars that are more massive than our sun. These stars have a higher proportion of heavy elements. This results in planets that are more massive, though fewer in number. However, these larger worlds orbiting their stars can gravitationally interact, causing instabilities. So the planets that survive these interactions are more likely to have varied orbits. To sum up this family: host stars more massive than our sun, with more heavy elements; planets fewer but more massive; orbits wider and more varied; dynamic history violent with possible collisions and ejections.

The wider planetary systems are more violent and chaotic, with planetary collisions and ejections. Danae Polychroni of the Italian National Institute for Astrophysics explained: “The scenarios that best reproduce the dynamical properties of the family of wide and massive systems are those in which the systems are unstable from birth. The greater abundance of material from which planets form around their host stars causes them to be born too large, preventing the system from remaining stable.”

Co-author Diego Turrini of the International Space Science Institute said: “The fundamental question we started from was: What determines the characteristics of planetary systems as we see them today? Are they established at birth or sculpted by evolution over time? The truth, in the end, turned out to lie somewhere in the middle. Our results show that there is a genetic link between planetary systems and their stars, and that, depending on the star around which they are born, this link also determines their fate.”

Co-author Camilla Danielski of the University of Valencia said: “The main difficulty during the data analysis was determining whether the two families of planetary systems were real or just an illusion created by the technological limits of current observational techniques. At the end of the path, it became clear that selection effects acted as the tip of the iceberg, revealing the existence of much deeper structural diversities between these systems.”

The astronomers hope that understanding which stars create which planetary systems will help in the search for planets that could be home to life.
