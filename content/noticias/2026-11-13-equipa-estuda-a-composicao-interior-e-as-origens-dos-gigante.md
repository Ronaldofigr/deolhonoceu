As medições de um isótopo de árgon poderão ajudar os cientistas a compreender se o interior de Úrano e de Neptuno, os chamados "gigantes gelados" do nosso Sistema Solar, é dominado por gelo ou por rocha. Uma nova investigação liderada pelo SwRI (Southwest Research Institute) e publicada na revista The Planetary Science Journal mediu o árgon-40 presente na atmosfera destes planetas para estimar a quantidade de rocha existente sob as suas superfícies e procurar pistas sobre as origens dos planetas, incluindo onde e como se formaram.

"Apesar de décadas de investigação, ainda dispomos de surpreendentemente pouca informação sobre o que se encontra por baixo das atmosferas de Úrano e Neptuno e sobre as suas composições físicas", afirmou o Dr. Olivier Mousis, cientista planetário da Divisão de Ciência e Exploração do Sistema Solar do SwRI, em Boulder, no estado norte-americano do Colorado, que liderou o estudo. "Vale a pena considerar se estes planetas poderão conter muito mais rocha do que as suas classificações como gigantes gelados sugerem. Investigámos se o árgon-40 nos poderia ajudar a distinguir entre estas possibilidades, ou se existem melhores utilizações para este marcador químico único em investigações futuras".

O árgon-40 encontrado nas atmosferas de Úrano e Neptuno tem duas origens possíveis. Parte do árgon-40 atmosférico poderá ter sido herdado da nebulosa protossolar, a nuvem de poeira e gás que formou o Sistema Solar há aproximadamente 4,6 mil milhões de anos. O árgon-40 adicional poderia ser explicado por milhares de milhões de anos de decaimento radioativo do potássio-40 encontrado em materiais rochosos nas profundezas dos planetas. "Com base na nossa investigação, o árgon primordial que chegou a Úrano e a Neptuno, bem como os seus isótopos, superariam completamente quaisquer contribuições resultantes do decaimento radioativo no interior dos planetas", afirmou Mousis, acrescentando que as contribuições de árgon-40 provenientes da nebulosa protossolar são mais abundantes do que se pensava anteriormente. Measurements of an argon isotope may help scientists understand whether the interior of Uranus and Neptune, the so-called “ice giants” of our Solar System, is dominated by ice or by rock. New research led by the Southwest Research Institute (SwRI) and published in The Planetary Science Journal measured argon-40 in the atmospheres of these planets to estimate how much rock lies beneath their surfaces and to seek clues about the planets’ origins, including where and how they formed.

“Despite decades of research, we still have surprisingly little information about what lies beneath the atmospheres of Uranus and Neptune and about their physical compositions,” said Dr. Olivier Mousis, a planetary scientist in SwRI’s Solar System Science and Exploration Division in Boulder, Colorado, who led the study. “It is worth considering whether these planets might contain far more rock than their classification as ice giants suggests. We investigated whether argon-40 could help us distinguish between these possibilities, or whether there are better uses for this unique chemical tracer in future investigations.”

The argon-40 found in the atmospheres of Uranus and Neptune has two possible origins. Some of the atmospheric argon-40 may have been inherited from the protosolar nebula, the cloud of dust and gas that formed the Solar System about 4.6 billion years ago. Additional argon-40 could be explained by billions of years of radioactive decay of potassium-40 found in rocky materials deep inside the planets. By distinguishing between the proportions of argon-40 that the planets inherited during their formation and the argon-40 produced in their interiors that reached their atmospheres, the team thought it could potentially extrapolate how much rock each planet contains.

The main challenge faced by the team was determining exactly how to distinguish between the two possible origins of the isotope. To solve this problem, Mousis and his team combined calculations of argon-40 production with a numerical model of the protosolar nebula. The model tracked the condensation, trapping and transport of “primordial” argon inside ice particles as the nebula evolved and that was later accreted to help form Uranus and Neptune.

“Based on our research, the primordial argon that reached Uranus and Neptune, as well as their isotopes, would completely overwhelm any contributions resulting from radioactive decay inside the planets,” said Mousis, adding that argon-40 contributions from the protosolar nebula are more abundant than previously thought. “With this in mind, we conclude that argon-40 measurements in the atmosphere likely tell us more about the formation environments and history of the planets than about their rocky or icy interiors.”

The team’s simulations revealed that the materials that gave rise to planets particularly rich in argon may have formed near an argon ice line, a boundary near the edge of the protosolar nebula where low temperatures condense argon gas into solid ice. The accumulation of of argon-bearing particles near the ice line may have influenced the chemical composition of the materials that formed Uranus and Neptune.

“Understanding the composition of the material that gave rise to Uranus and Neptune is essential for reconstructing their formation histories,” said Dr. Robin Canup, vice president of SwRI’s Solar System Science and Exploration Division and co-author of the study. “Our results demonstrate how evolving conditions in the early Solar System could leave chemical signatures that persist in planetary atmospheres billions of years later.”

These findings may help guide future studies of the atmospheres of Uranus and Neptune. According to the researchers, future probe missions, such as NASA’s proposed Uranus Orbiter and Probe mission, could directly sample noble gases and isotopic ratios that cannot be reliably determined through remote observations. Measurements of nitrogen, carbon and other atmospheric components could help determine the validity of theories about how the ice giants formed.

“Argon-40 measurements can help us answer two fundamental questions that have long eluded scientists,” said Mousis. “What are Uranus and Neptune made of, and where did their components form before being incorporated into the planets? Future atmospheric probes equipped with sufficiently sensitive mass spectrometers could bring us closer to answering both questions.”
