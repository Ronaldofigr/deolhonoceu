Just 4.24 light-years away, orbiting the tiny red dwarf star Proxima Centauri, is the planet Proxima b. It is the closest known exoplanet to the Solar System. Discovered in 2016 using the radial velocity method, it has remained one of the most intriguing targets in the search for life beyond Earth.

The planet has roughly Earth’s mass (minimum about 1.06–1.3 Earth masses) and orbits within its star’s habitable zone — at a distance where liquid water could exist on the surface if it has a suitable atmosphere. Its orbital period is only about 11.2 days, so a year on Proxima b lasts less than two weeks, and the planet is likely tidally locked: one side permanently faces the star, the other remains in eternal night.

In 2016, an atmosphere of hydrogen and helium was not “detected” in the literal sense. Scientists immediately began modeling possible atmospheric compositions and discussing whether the planet could retain water and gases despite the powerful flares and stellar wind of its active red dwarf. To this day, Proxima b’s atmosphere remains unconfirmed: the planet does not pass in front of its star (it does not transit), so the classic transmission spectroscopy used by the James Webb Space Telescope cannot be applied here.

Research continues. The James Webb Space Telescope and ground-based observatories are trying to capture the planet’s thermal emission (phase curves) and look for indirect signs of an atmosphere. Future instruments (including projects like RISTRETTO) may finally be able to directly observe the reflected light of Proxima b and check whether it has oxygen, water vapor, or something entirely different.

In short: this is the closest chance we have to look at a world where liquid water could theoretically exist. The key remaining question is whether any atmosphere survived the star’s onslaught. That is exactly why Proxima b remains one of the most exciting targets in modern astronomy.
