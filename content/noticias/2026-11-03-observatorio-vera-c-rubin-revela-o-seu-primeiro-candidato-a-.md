Com observações realizadas durante a fase preparatória do LSST (Legacy Survey of Space and Time), o Observatório Vera C. Rubin descobriu um novo candidato a planeta anão no nosso Sistema Solar. O candidato, ao qual foi atribuída a designação provisória 2026 ED138 (ED138), foi descoberto por Matthew Holman (Centro de Astrofísica | Harvard & Smithsonian), membro da equipa de operações do Rubin e colaborador que contribui para melhorar os algoritmos que procuram objetos distantes em movimento no nosso Sistema Solar. A distância do candidato em relação ao Sol situa-o para além da órbita do planeta anão Plutão. A análise inicial indica que poderá estar entre os corpos maiores e mais brilhantes que os cientistas já descobriram nesta região do Sistema Solar.

"Esse trunfo amplia consideravelmente a nossa capacidade de revelar e compreender novos aspetos sobre o nosso Sistema Solar, a Via Láctea e milhares de milhões de outras galáxias em todo o Universo".

"Os pontos fortes e únicos do Observatório Rubin tornam-no uma ferramenta incrível para investigar uma vasta gama de fenómenos astrofísicos, desde a energia escura até à descoberta deste candidato a planeta anão", afirma Kathy Turner, gestora do programa do Departamento de Energia dos EUA para o Observatório Rubin.

"A minha primeira reação foi de incredulidade entusiasmada", afirma Holman. "Objetos como este são muito raros, por isso comecei imediatamente a verificar se eu ou o código tínhamos cometido algum erro".

Para eliminar qualquer dúvida, iniciou-se uma pesquisa detalhada dos dados, na esperança de descobrir mais observações de ED138. A título de referência, o planeta anão Plutão tem uma distância média de 40 UA. Isto classifica ED138 como um objeto transneptuniano (OTN) - objetos que orbitam o Sol a uma distância superior à de Neptuno.

Os cientistas estudam o tamanho, a composição e as trajetórias orbitais dos OTNs para compreender como o Sistema Solar foi formado, como os planetas gigantes migraram ao longo do tempo e como os objetos mais pequenos foram dispersos ao longo desta evolução. A órbita de ED138 está inclinada quase 25 graus em relação ao plano do Sistema Solar, o que o classifica como um OTN clássico "quente" e sugere que foi desviado da sua órbita original devido a interações gravitacionais com Neptuno.

Nem todos os OTNs são considerados candidatos a planeta anão, sendo a distinção feita com base no tamanho e na forma do objeto. Para ser considerado um planeta anão, um objeto tem de ter massa suficiente para que a sua própria gravidade lhe confira uma forma esférica. Com base na distância, no brilho e na refletividade superficial estimada (albedo) de ED138, a equipa estima que o seu tamanho se situe entre 600 e 1500 quilómetros, e espera-se que corpos gelados desta dimensão sejam redondos.

Esta análise preliminar coloca ED138 entre os maiores candidatos a planeta anão alguma vez descobertos para lá de Neptuno, embora sejam necessárias observações no infravermelho para determinar o seu tamanho exato e confirmar a sua natureza como planeta anão. Se confirmado, ED138 juntar-se-á aos cinco planetas anões reconhecidos pela União Astronómica Internacional: Plutão, Ceres, Éris, Haumea e Makemake.

"O Rubin está a proporcionar-nos uma capacidade sem precedentes para explorar o céu através de uma combinação de velocidade, profundidade e precisão", afirma Jurić. "E a descoberta é apenas o início: assim que identificamos um novo objeto, podemos pesquisar imagens com meses, e por vezes com décadas, encontrando-o em imagens onde ninguém sabia que devia procurar. "Se for confirmado como planeta anão, ED138 passará a fazer parte de uma família muito pequena de mundos", afirma Holman. "O Rubin irá captar imagens de todo o céu austral repetidamente durante dez anos, pelo que quaisquer outros que se escondam em locais que os levantamentos anteriores tenham ignorado estão agora ao nosso alcance".

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory has detected, in the outer reaches of our Solar System, beyond the orbit of Pluto, 2026 ED138 — a large and bright trans-Neptunian object that scientists have identified as a dwarf planet candidate. The observations were announced by the Minor Planet Center of the International Astronomical Union. If confirmed, ED138 will rank among the largest dwarf planets ever discovered in this region of the Solar System.

With observations carried out during the preparatory phase of the LSST (Legacy Survey of Space and Time), the Vera C. Rubin Observatory discovered a new dwarf planet candidate in our Solar System. The candidate, which was given the provisional designation 2026 ED138 (ED138), was discovered by Matthew Holman (Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian), a member of the Rubin operations team and a collaborator who contributes to improving the algorithms that search for distant moving objects in our Solar System. The candidate’s distance from the Sun places it beyond the orbit of the dwarf planet Pluto. Initial analysis indicates that it may be among the larger and brighter bodies that scientists have already discovered in this region of the Solar System.

"The great strength of the Rubin Observatory is the ability to clearly capture the motion and change among a vast number of objects and phenomena that humans have never seen before," says Edward Ajhar, NSF program director for the Rubin Observatory. "That strength greatly expands our ability to reveal and understand new aspects of our Solar System, the Milky Way and billions of other galaxies throughout the Universe."

"The unique strengths of the Rubin Observatory make it an incredible tool for investigating a wide range of astrophysical phenomena, from dark energy to the discovery of this dwarf planet candidate," says Kathy Turner, U.S. Department of Energy program manager for the Rubin Observatory.

The IAU Minor Planet Center announced the discovery in an Electronic Circular on Minor Planets. Mario Jurić, a faculty member at the University of Washington and chief scientist at the Rubin Observatory for the Solar System, and Holman presented the results at a press conference at the 58th annual meeting of the Division for Planetary Sciences in Spokane, Washington, USA.

On the night of October 13, Holman was analyzing data collected by Rubin in early 2026 using a search code he developed with his colleague from the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian, Kevin Napier. The code was designed to analyze billions of detections of moving objects, compare them and identify the detections that correspond to the same object. Using this method, Holman discovered ED138 and immediately realized that its brightness and distance indicated that it was an unusually large object.

"My first reaction was one of excited disbelief," says Holman. "Objects like this are very rare, so I immediately began checking whether I or the code had made any mistake."

To eliminate any doubt, a detailed search of the data was begun, in the hope of discovering more observations of ED138. Jurić searched the Rubin catalogs and images and found another 20 observations, dating back to March 4, 2026. In total, the Rubin Simonyi Survey Telescope observed ED138 30 times over 20 nights, between March 4 and July 11. Additional archival images from the DECam (Dark Energy Camera), manufactured by the U.S. Department of Energy, and discoveries by the ADAM Precovery service of the Asteroid Institute, provided data dating back to 2016. These data allowed the team to track more than 10 years of ED138’s orbit. DECam is mounted on the NSF’s 4-meter Victor M. Blanco Telescope at CTIO (Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory), a program of the NSF’s NOIRLab (National Optical-Infrared Astronomy Research Laboratory).

ED138 is currently at a distance of about 46 astronomical units (AU), where 1 AU corresponds to the average distance between Earth and the Sun. For reference, the dwarf planet Pluto has an average distance of 40 AU. This classifies ED138 as a trans-Neptunian object (TNO) — objects that orbit the Sun at a distance greater than that of Neptune.

Scientists study the size, composition and orbital trajectories of TNOs to understand how the Solar System was formed, how the giant planets migrated over time and how the smaller objects were scattered throughout this evolution. ED138’s orbit is inclined almost 25 degrees relative to the plane of the Solar System, which classifies it as a "hot" classical TNO and suggests that it was diverted from its original orbit due to gravitational interactions with Neptune.

Not all TNOs are considered dwarf planet candidates; the distinction is made on the basis of the size and shape of the object. To be considered a dwarf planet, an object must have enough mass for its own gravity to give it a spherical shape. Based on the distance, brightness and estimated surface reflectivity (albedo) of ED138, the team estimates that its size is between 600 and 1500 kilometers, and icy bodies of this size are expected to be round.

This preliminary analysis places ED138 among the largest dwarf planet candidates ever discovered beyond Neptune, although infrared observations are needed to determine its exact size and confirm its nature as a dwarf planet. If confirmed, ED138 will join the five dwarf planets recognized by the International Astronomical Union: Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea and Makemake.

ED138 was found in a region of the sky near the plane of the Milky Way — a region densely populated by millions of stars. For most telescopes, ED138 went unnoticed among the myriad of background sources, which likely explains why it has only now been discovered. The regular and continuous monitoring of the entire southern hemisphere sky by the Rubin Observatory, combined with powerful algorithms that analyze millions to billions of points of light to identify those that correspond to small moving objects, allowed the team to detect ED138 in that star-filled field.

"Rubin is providing us with an unprecedented ability to explore the sky through a combination of speed, depth and precision," says Jurić. "And the discovery is just the beginning: once we identify a new object, we can search images months, and sometimes decades old, finding it in images where no one knew they should look. This ability to discover and ‘rediscover’ objects is transforming the speed and precision with which we can determine their orbits and understand the distant Solar System."

ED138 joins the rapidly growing sample of Solar System objects discovered by Rubin since it began collecting the first data, in preparation for the LSST, which began in June 2026. In April, before the formal start of the LSST, the Rubin Solar System team submitted observations of more than 11,000 new asteroids to the IAU Minor Planet Center, including about 380 new TNOs. At the time, the team referred to this vast data set as "the tip of the iceberg" and expressed their enthusiasm for the discoveries that would follow once the LSST came into operation. With the discovery of ED138, those hopes are being realized.

"If it is confirmed as a dwarf planet, ED138 will become part of a very small family of worlds," says Holman. "Rubin will capture images of the entire southern sky repeatedly for ten years, so any others that are hiding in places that previous surveys have overlooked are now within our reach."
