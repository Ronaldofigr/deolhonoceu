Estamos num período de atividade frenética para os cientistas que procuram vida em planetas distantes. Até à data, os astrónomos catalogaram mais de 6000 planetas para lá do nosso Sistema Solar e estão a analisar as atmosferas dos mundos mais promissores em busca de substâncias químicas que possam ser subprodutos da vida. Os astrobiólogos estão particularmente interessados em atmosferas semelhantes à da Terra, que retêm calor suficiente para permitir a existência de água líquida na superfície do planeta, que os cientistas consideram o fator-chave para determinar se um planeta pode abrigar vida tal como a conhecemos.

Mas os cientistas também querem distinguir entre mundos habitáveis e mundos habitados. Para tal, precisam de saber que tipos de condições atmosféricas num planeta podem ser explicadas sem a existência de vida, para que possam identificar planetas com atmosferas que só podem ser explicadas pela influência da vida.

Para fornecer essa referência planetária, investigadores da Universidade de Washington construíram uma Terra virtual complexa com uma peculiaridade crucial: sem vida.

Num artigo científico publicado a 18 de setembro na revista The Planetary Science Journal, os investigadores simularam os sistemas geoquímicos da Terra ao longo de 5 mil milhões de anos - desde a sua formação, há 4,5 mil milhões de anos, até cerca de 500 milhões de anos no futuro. A simulação modelou a troca de substâncias químicas entre o núcleo, o manto, a crosta, a superfície e a atmosfera ao longo do tempo. Previu com precisão 19 propriedades planetárias, como o fluxo de calor através do manto, mas não teve em conta a influência da vida nesses sistemas. A simulação pode ser generalizada para prever como uma atmosfera poderá evoluir em qualquer planeta rochoso e pode ajudar a identificar indícios de vida em atmosferas que não se enquadram no modelo.

"Este modelo pode ajudar-nos a perceber como planetas habitáveis, mas sem vida, podem assemelhar-se a planetas com vida", afirmou a autora principal, Samantha Gilbert-Janizek, que concluiu esta investigação enquanto doutoranda em astronomia na Universidade de Washington e é agora bolseira pós-doc na Universidade da Califórnia em Riverside. "Quando interpretarmos observações da atmosfera de um exoplaneta, esta ferramenta irá ajudar-nos a determinar a probabilidade de que o que observamos indique a existência de vida na superfície".

Versões anteriores do modelo simulavam o núcleo, o manto, a crosta e a radiação estelar, mas não incluíam a superfície nem a atmosfera.

We are in a period of frenetic activity for scientists searching for life on distant planets. To date, astronomers have catalogued more than 6,000 planets beyond our Solar System and are analysing the atmospheres of the most promising worlds for chemicals that could be by-products of life. Astrobiologists are particularly interested in Earth-like atmospheres that retain enough heat to allow liquid water on the planet’s surface, which scientists consider the key factor in determining whether a planet can harbour life as we know it.

But scientists also want to distinguish between habitable worlds and inhabited worlds. To do so, they need to know what kinds of atmospheric conditions on a planet can be explained without the existence of life, so that they can identify planets with atmospheres that can only be explained by the influence of life.

To provide that planetary reference, researchers at the University of Washington built a complex virtual Earth with a crucial peculiarity: no life.

In a scientific paper published on 18 September in The Planetary Science Journal, the researchers simulated Earth’s geochemical systems over 5 billion years — from its formation 4.5 billion years ago to about 500 million years into the future. The simulation modelled the exchange of chemical substances between the core, mantle, crust, surface and atmosphere over time. It accurately predicted 19 planetary properties, such as heat flow through the mantle, but did not take into account the influence of life on those systems. The simulation can be generalised to predict how an atmosphere might evolve on any rocky planet and can help identify signs of life in atmospheres that do not fit the model.

"This model can help us understand how habitable but lifeless planets might resemble planets with life," said lead author Samantha Gilbert-Janizek, who completed this research while a PhD student in astronomy at the University of Washington and is now a postdoctoral fellow at the University of California, Riverside. "When we interpret observations of an exoplanet’s atmosphere, this tool will help us determine the probability that what we observe indicates the existence of life on the surface."

Gilbert-Janizek’s work builds on a model developed in the laboratory of senior author Rory Barnes, associate professor of astronomy at the University of Washington. Previous versions of the model simulated the core, mantle, crust and stellar radiation, but did not include the surface or the atmosphere.

A planet’s atmosphere is shaped by many non-biological factors, including heat and gases from the interior and from the star. But it is also in the atmosphere of a distant planet that astronomers hope to find concrete evidence of life.

There are many ways in which life can alter an atmosphere: on Earth, plants and animals release constant fluxes of oxygen and methane into the atmosphere. Those two elements react with each other when mixed. Thus an extraterrestrial astronomer studying our planet might suspect that something living was replenishing the reserves of both gases.

That logic led human astronomer Gilbert-Janizek to add surface and atmosphere modelling to the existing Earth simulation, completing the picture of how chemical substances move through all of Earth’s systems.

The result is a model that can be adjusted to study any rocky exoplanet that astrobiologists find interesting. Researchers can enter basic details about that planet and run the simulation to check whether the model can reproduce the observed atmosphere without the influence of life. If they cannot obtain a match, the planet in question becomes even more interesting.

"Dr Gilbert-Janizek’s work represents an exciting step towards understanding where life may exist in the Universe," said Barnes. "After more than a decade of work by many scientists, Dr Gilbert-Janizek has finally completed a model of Earth that simultaneously reproduces the properties of Earth’s core, mantle, surface and atmosphere. That calibration gives us the confidence that future exoplanet simulations will be accurate."

Gilbert-Janizek says the new and improved model can also help guide the design of future telescopes built to study distant planets, such as the proposed HWO (Habitable Worlds Observatory).

"Achieving the ambitious goals of a telescope like the Habitable Worlds Observatory requires decades of work across many generations of scientists," said Gilbert-Janizek. "I am very grateful to be part of this lineage and, one day, to pass the baton to the next generation, so that we can continue to advance in answering this question: whether or not we are alone in the Universe."
