Agora, num novo estudo, os investigadores demonstram que um tipo específico de estrela, designada por estrela supermassiva, pode explicar as características surpreendentes dos LRDs, incluindo os seus espetros invulgares, a sua aparência compacta no céu e a sua abundância de azoto.

"Tanto quanto sei, é o primeiro modelo capaz de explicar muitas das propriedades observadas de uma só vez, desde os espetros à morfologia e às assinaturas químicas", afirmou Devesh Nandal, do HCO (Harvard College Observatory), que faz parte do Centro de Astrofísica | Harvard & Smithsonian. "Até os cenários concorrentes estão agora a invocar as estrelas supermassivas como o motor central".

Os LRDs são fontes de luz compactas, vermelhas e extremamente brilhantes, e o que quer que esteja a emitir a luz já existia quando o Universo tinha menos de mil milhões de anos. O seu espetro de luz revela a presença de hidrogénio de formas que os atuais modelos astronómicos de galáxias jovens ou de núcleos galácticos ativos comuns não conseguem explicar.

No entanto, muitos LRDs observados não apresentam a forte emissão de raios X ou de ondas de rádio que os astrónomos esperariam de um buraco negro supermassivo em crescimento.

"Os pequenos pontos vermelhos são misteriosos porque combinam pistas que normalmente não se encaixam", afirmou Nandal. "Parecem estar a dizer-nos que algo muito luminoso está escondido no interior de gás denso".

Num trabalho anterior, Nandal e os seus colaboradores demonstraram que estrelas gigantes, com massas cerca de 100.000 vezes superiores à do Sol, podem reproduzir espetros-chave muito semelhantes aos dos LRDs, incluindo padrões invulgares de hidrogénio.

Astronomers have long speculated about the mysterious "Little Red Dots" (LRDs) observed in images of the early Universe captured by the James Webb Space Telescope: they could be galaxies, black holes, quasi-stars, starbursts... or something completely different.

Now, in a new study, researchers show that a specific type of star, called a supermassive star, can explain the surprising characteristics of LRDs, including their unusual spectra, compact appearance in the sky and abundance of nitrogen.

"To my knowledge, it is the first model capable of explaining many of the observed properties at once, from the spectra to the morphology and chemical signatures," said Devesh Nandal of the Harvard College Observatory (HCO), part of the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian. "Even competing scenarios are now invoking supermassive stars as the central engine."

LRDs are compact, red and extremely bright light sources, and whatever is emitting the light already existed when the Universe was less than a billion years old. Their light spectrum reveals the presence of hydrogen in ways that current astronomical models of young galaxies or ordinary active galactic nuclei cannot explain.

However, many observed LRDs do not show the strong X-ray or radio emission that astronomers would expect from a growing supermassive black hole.

"Little red dots are mysterious because they combine clues that do not usually fit together," said Nandal. "They seem to be telling us that something very luminous is hidden inside dense gas."

In previous work, Nandal and his collaborators showed that giant stars, with masses around 100,000 times that of the Sun, can reproduce key spectra very similar to those of LRDs, including unusual hydrogen patterns.

The new study seeks to determine whether these stars can also create the dense gas "cocoons" that make LRDs appear so compact in James Webb images.

The researchers tracked the life cycles and evolution of some known giant stars and analyzed how they appear, through our telescopes, to flicker and lose mass. They discovered that these stars do not lose mass in the final phase of their lives, as happens with many other stars. Instead, they undergo discrete and powerful pulsation episodes that the researchers call "strange mode." This unusual behavior ejects shell-like gas structures.

"The spectrum and the morphology are two facets of the same physical problem," said Nandal. "The spectrum tells us what kind of source is producing the light and how that light is processed, while the structure tells us where the surrounding material is and how compact it must be."

Based on their models, the researchers show that a single supermassive star can create both the spectral signatures and the compact gas layer observed in LRDs.

The researchers note that the ejected material is mainly hydrogen and helium, but also contains nitrogen. Observations of LRDs are beginning to reveal similar nitrogen-rich spectra, which constitutes further evidence in favor of their giant-star theory.

After their last ejection, these stars continue to evolve until they undergo direct collapse, forming the seed of a supermassive black hole.

"What I find most fascinating is that this result brings together many independent clues into a single physical framework," said Nandal.

The team's next goal is to turn these properties into full predictions for the detailed spectra of LRDs, so that James Webb observations can directly test their theory.
