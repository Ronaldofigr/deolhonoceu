## O que é a matéria escura?

A matéria escura constitui a maior parte da matéria do Universo — cerca de **27%** do conteúdo cósmico, face a apenas ~5% de matéria comum (estrelas, planetas, gás). O resto é energia escura. Interage com a matéria comum pela gravidade, mas não parece interagir com o espectro eletromagnético: não absorve, reflete nem emite luz. Por isso não a “vemos” diretamente; inferimo-la pelos efeitos gravitacionais em galáxias e aglomerados.

Detetar objetos escuros de massa muito baixa é crucial: diferentes teorias da matéria escura (por exemplo, matéria escura fria vs. quente ou modelos de partículas leves) preveem populações distintas de pequenos aglomerados. Quanto mais baixo for o limite de massa dos objetos detetados, mais se restringem esses modelos.

A combinação de lentes gravitacionais de alta precisão e modelagem independente abre caminho para um “censo” de estruturas escuras de baixa massa — e, com isso, para pistas mais sólidas sobre a verdadeira natureza da cola invisível do cosmos.

A mysterious body has drawn the interest of the astronomical community: it is the **lowest-mass dark object** yet found. The hope is that it will help us understand the nature of **dark matter** — the “invisible glue” that holds the Universe together, in NASA’s words.

According to a paper in *Nature*, the object is so hard to see that the discovery was made with a **non-parametric gravitational imaging** technique, confirmed by independent parametric modelling.

BBC *Sky at Night* described it as a dark-matter clump **100 times smaller** than any previously detected. Another possibility is that it is a **compact, inactive dwarf galaxy**.

“It is an impressive feat to detect such a low-mass object at such a great distance from us,” said Chris Fassnacht, a professor in the Department of Physics and Astronomy at the University of California and one of the study’s authors. “Objects like this are fundamental to understanding the nature of dark matter.”

## What is dark matter?

Dark matter makes up most of the matter in the Universe — about **27%** of the cosmic content, versus only ~5% ordinary matter (stars, planets, gas). The rest is dark energy. It interacts with ordinary matter through gravity but does not appear to interact with the electromagnetic spectrum: it neither absorbs, reflects nor emits light. So we do not “see” it directly; we infer it from gravitational effects on galaxies and clusters.

Detecting very low-mass dark objects is crucial: different dark-matter theories (for example cold vs. warm dark matter, or light-particle models) predict different populations of small clumps. The lower the mass limit of detected objects, the more those models are constrained.

The combination of high-precision gravitational lensing and independent modelling opens the way to a “census” of low-mass dark structures — and, with it, to stronger clues about the true nature of the cosmos’s invisible glue.
